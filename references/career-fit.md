# From a career goal to course topics

These are starting hypotheses for researching course descriptions, not required course lists, a hiring standard,
or a claim that any particular university offers these subjects. Adapt them to the student's role and interests.
Map topics to real local courses only after reading the institution's published descriptions.

## Machine learning engineer

Ask whether the student is most interested in building/deploying models, data infrastructure, or research.
Potential foundations: programming, data structures and algorithms, linear algebra, probability, and statistics.
Potential applications: machine learning, data management, software engineering, model evaluation, and responsible
use of data. Systems, distributed computing, and deployment-oriented coursework may fit an engineering focus;
advanced mathematical topics may fit a research focus. Verify local prerequisites rather than assuming the
introductory ML course is immediately accessible. Do not equate one ML elective with complete job preparation.

## Network engineer

Clarify networking operations/infrastructure versus distributed software, network security, or telecom hardware.
Possible topics: computer networks, operating systems, systems administration, scripting, network security,
distributed systems, and cloud infrastructure. A class about biological or social networks may be a poor match:
read its description rather than matching only the word "networks." Certifications and vendor training are
separate options and are not university degree requirements unless the program explicitly says so.

## Other careers

Use the same method for any role or major:
1. Clarify the work the student wants to do, beyond a possibly ambiguous title.
2. Find relevant official career guidance, professional-body material, or university career pathways when needed.
3. Identify a few topic areas and map them to published local course learning outcomes.
4. Prioritize courses that also satisfy remaining degree requirements and unlock useful later courses.
5. Explain each connection as a recommendation, cite its basis, and offer an alternative focus if appropriate.

If a regulated profession is involved, verify the relevant jurisdiction's official accreditation or licensing
requirements before making eligibility claims. Do not promise that a course selection satisfies professional
licensing requirements or replace a university's formal program approval.
